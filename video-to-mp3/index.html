    How to Use

    1. Drop a video or audio file into the area above. Its name, size and length appear right away. On first use the on-device engine (~31 MB) downloads once and is cached — after that it starts instantly.
    2. Pick a bitrate. 128 kbps is right for speech, 192 kbps for music, 320 kbps for an archival copy. Tick Mono for interviews and lectures to roughly halve the file size.
    3. Click Extract MP3. The progress bar and the button both show the real percentage, and when encoding finishes the file downloads as filename.mp3.

    Why pulling audio out of your own recordings is so useful

    Most of what people record is really audio wearing a video costume. A two-hour lecture capture, a webinar, a rehearsal video, a phone clip of a meeting — you rarely need the picture again, but you do want the sound on your phone, in a podcast app, or in a transcription tool. Stripping the video track turns a 900 MB recording into a file you can actually keep and carry around, and MP3 is the one audio format that plays everywhere without arguing.

    Honest note: this is a re-encode, not a copy. Unlike trimming, every second of audio has to be decoded and encoded again, so the time scales with the length of the file — seconds for a short clip, minutes for a multi-hour recording, and slower on a phone than on a laptop. Memory is the other real limit: because nothing is sent to a server, the work happens in your browser's memory, so very large sources cap out around 1–2 GB on a desktop and lower on mobile. That is the trade we make in exchange for your files never leaving the device.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is my video really never uploaded?

    Correct. The tool downloads FFmpeg compiled to WebAssembly (about 31 MB, once, then cached) and extracts the audio inside the browser. Nothing is transmitted, so a lecture recording, a private meeting, or an unreleased interview never touches anyone else's server — and there is no upload wait before work starts.

    Why does this take longer than trimming a video?

    Trimming can copy the existing stream, but MP3 has to be encoded from scratch, so every second of audio is actually processed. The time is roughly proportional to length: a few seconds for a short clip, a minute or two for a typical lecture, several minutes for a multi-hour recording on a phone. The progress bar shows the real position, so leave the tab open until it finishes. Memory is the other limit — processing happens in your browser, so very large sources top out around 1-2 GB on a desktop and less on phones.

    Which bitrate should I choose?

    128 kbps is plenty for speech — lectures, meetings, interviews, voice memos — and keeps files small. 192 kbps is the sweet spot for music and podcasts with background scoring. 320 kbps is for archival copies where you would rather spend the disk space than lose detail. For pure speech, also tick Mono: it roughly halves the file with no audible loss.

    Can I convert audio files too, like M4A or WAV to MP3?

    Yes. Audio files are accepted the same way as video, so M4A from a voice recorder, AAC, or a bulky WAV can be turned into a compact MP3 that plays on almost anything. When the input is already audio there is no video track to discard, so the conversion is usually quicker than a video of the same length.

    Is it OK to extract audio from any video?

    Only use this on content you own or otherwise have the right to use — your own recordings, lectures you are permitted to keep, material you have a licence for, or public domain works. Ripping audio from someone else's film, show, or music video is a copyright matter in most countries. Because everything runs on your device, the tool cannot and does not check what you feed it, so that judgement is yours.
