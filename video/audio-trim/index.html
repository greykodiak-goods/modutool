How to Use

  1. Drop an audio file into the area above (video files work too — the audio is extracted). On first use the on-device engine (~31 MB) downloads once and is cached, so later visits start instantly.
  2. Play the preview and set the Start and End sliders. The player jumps to the slider position as you drag, and the clip length is shown underneath so you can hit a target like 30 seconds exactly.
  3. Choose an Output. Same format copies the section without re-encoding — instant and lossless. MP3 re-encodes at 192 kbps for maximum compatibility. Tick Fade to soften the start and end.
  4. Click Trim & Download. The file saves as filename_trim with the matching extension.

Why trimming audio in the browser makes sense

Cutting thirty seconds out of a song or a recording is a two-click job, yet the usual options are a full audio editor you have to install or an upload site you have to trust with the file. This tool ships FFmpeg — the engine underneath most professional audio pipelines — compiled to WebAssembly, so the cut happens on your device. Nothing is transmitted, which matters more than people expect: voice memos, interview recordings, client calls and unreleased mixes are exactly the sort of thing you should not be handing to a random server.

Honest note on speed and precision: Same format is a stream copy, so it is lossless and finishes almost instantly, but cuts land on audio frame boundaries and can shift by a few tens of milliseconds. That is far less noticeable than the keyframe snapping you get when trimming video, though it is still not sample-exact. Choosing MP3 or enabling fade re-encodes the clip, which takes real time proportional to the clip length. And because the work happens in browser memory, very large sources cap out around 1–2 GB on a desktop and less on phones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my audio really never uploaded?

Correct. The tool downloads FFmpeg compiled to WebAssembly (about 31 MB, once, then cached) and cuts the file inside the browser. Nothing is transmitted, so voice memos, interview tapes, therapy notes or unreleased tracks stay on your machine — and there is no upload wait before the cut begins.

Can I make an iPhone ringtone with this?

Partly, and here is the honest answer. This tool gives you an MP3 or a clip in the original format — it does not produce the .m4r file iOS expects, so for an iPhone you still need to convert the clip to m4r and load it through your library. On Android it is straightforward: trim to roughly 20-30 seconds, save the MP3, put it in the Ringtones folder on your phone, and pick it in the sound settings.

Why is Same format instant while MP3 takes a while?

Same format uses a stream copy: the chosen section is lifted out byte for byte with no decoding, so it is lossless and finishes almost immediately. Choosing MP3, or turning on fade, means the audio has to be decoded and encoded again, which is real work and scales with the length of the clip. One caveat on stream copy: cuts land on frame boundaries, so the start can shift by a few tens of milliseconds. That is far smaller than the keyframe snapping you get with video, but it is not sample-exact.

What does the fade option do, and why is it sometimes greyed out?

Fade adds a 0.3 second fade in at the start and a 0.3 second fade out at the end, which removes the abrupt click you hear when a clip begins or ends mid-waveform. It needs room to work, so it is only available once your selection is longer than about 0.7 seconds. Because fading changes the samples themselves, turning it on always re-encodes the clip to MP3 even if you selected Same format.

Is there a file size or length limit?

We impose none, but everything runs in your browser's memory, so the practical ceiling depends on your device — roughly 1-2 GB on a desktop and less on phones. Audio files are usually far smaller than that, so in practice you will only hit it with very long lossless recordings. First use also downloads the roughly 31 MB engine once, after which it is cached and starts instantly.
