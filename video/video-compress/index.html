    What CRF actually means

    Most compressors ask you for a target size. This one asks for a target quality, which is what CRF (Constant Rate Factor) is: a number where lower means better looking and bigger, higher means smaller and softer. The encoder then spends whatever bitrate each scene needs to hit that quality — a still title card costs almost nothing, a handheld pan through leaves costs a lot.

    The honest consequence is that nobody can promise you an exact output size from a quality setting, us included. A static screen recording at CRF 28 can lose 90% of its size; shaky, grainy handheld footage at the same CRF might only lose 40%. That is why this page shows you the real before and after numbers instead of a prediction, and why switching levels and re-running is a perfectly normal workflow.

    Rough guide: CRF 23 is visually near-transparent for most content, CRF 28 is the sweet spot where the file is much smaller and almost nobody notices on a phone screen, and CRF 32 is visibly softer but still perfectly watchable — the right choice when getting under a limit matters more than pixel purity.

    Why downscaling beats CRF alone for phone videos

    A modern phone records at 1080p or 4K, but the video is usually going to be watched in a chat bubble, an email preview or a messaging app — often on the same phone screen it was shot with. Those pixels are being paid for and never seen.

    Because file size scales with the pixel count, halving the width quarters the pixels, and that cut compounds with whatever CRF is doing. Going from 4K to 1280 px wide typically saves more than any CRF change on its own. That is why the Recommended level caps the width at 1280 px and Strong caps it at 854 px, while Light leaves the resolution alone for footage you intend to keep or edit later. Videos already narrower than the cap are never upscaled — the cap only ever reduces.

    An honest note on limits

    • Nothing is uploaded. FFmpeg is compiled to WebAssembly and runs inside this page, so your footage never touches a server and there is no upload wait.
    • This is lossy. Re-encoding throws detail away permanently, and compressing an already-compressed file repeatedly compounds the damage. Keep your original.
    • It can fail to shrink. A file that is already small or heavily compressed can come out the same size or bigger. When that happens the tool refuses to download it and tells you to keep the original.
    • Very large files hit browser memory. We impose no cap, but the whole file is held in your browser's memory — roughly 1–2 GB on a desktop, considerably less on a phone.
    • Re-encoding is CPU-bound and single-threaded here, so a few minutes for a long clip is normal, not a bug.
    • Audio is re-encoded to AAC at 128 kbps, which is transparent for speech and fine for most music in a shared clip.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is my video really never uploaded?

    Correct. The tool downloads FFmpeg compiled to WebAssembly (about 31 MB, once, then cached) and re-encodes the video inside the browser. Nothing is transmitted, so there is no upload wait either — a 500 MB file starts processing immediately.

    What is CRF, and why can you not promise an exact output size?

    CRF is a quality target, not a size target. You tell the encoder how good the result has to look and it spends whatever bitrate that takes, scene by scene. A static screen recording at CRF 28 may shrink by 90 percent while shaky handheld footage at the same setting barely halves. That is why the tool shows you the real before and after sizes instead of promising a number up front.

    Why is the output always MP4, even for a WebM or MOV input?

    We re-encode to H.264 video with AAC 128 kbps audio in an MP4 container, because that combination plays practically everywhere — phones, browsers, chat apps, TVs and editing software. So a WebM or MOV input comes back as an MP4. The faststart flag is also set, so the file can begin playing before it has fully downloaded.

    How do I get under an email or chat size limit?

    Start with Recommended and look at the resulting size. Still too big? Try Strong, which also drops the resolution to 854 px wide. If it is still over the limit, shorten the clip first — length affects size more directly than any quality setting, and our trim tool cuts without re-encoding, so it costs nothing in quality or time.

    Why does it take minutes, and is there a size limit?

    Compressing means decoding every frame and encoding it again, and the engine runs single-threaded here, so expect roughly real time or slower on long clips — the progress bar and the button show the real percentage as it works. We impose no size limit, but everything happens in your browser's memory, so the practical ceiling is roughly 1–2 GB on a desktop and less on a phone.
