How to Use

  1. Drop your video into the area above. On first use the on-device engine (~31 MB) downloads once and is cached — after that it starts instantly.
  2. Play the preview and set the Start and End sliders. The preview jumps to the slider position as you drag. The end defaults to 6 seconds after the start, because short GIFs are good GIFs.

Why GIFs balloon in size — and what actually controls it

GIF is a 1987 format. It stores every frame as a separate image and has none of the tricks that make modern video small: no motion prediction, no inter-frame compression worth the name, and a hard ceiling of 256 colors per frame. The practical consequence is that a GIF's size is roughly width × height × frame rate × length, and all four of those multiply together. A 640 px, 15 fps, 20-second clip routinely lands past 30 MB — bigger than the MP4 it came from.

So the three controls on this page are the whole game. Length is the biggest lever: halving the clip halves the file. Width is next, and it hits twice, since dropping 640 px to 320 px quarters the pixel count. Frame rate is the gentlest — 12 fps looks fine for screen recordings and UI demos, and 8 fps is usually enough for a slow pan or a static talking head.

What the palette pass does

Because a GIF frame can only reference 256 colors, something has to decide which 256. The default answer is a generic web-safe palette, and it looks bad: skin tones go blotchy, gradients turn into visible stripes, and dark scenes fill with dithering noise.

The High (2-pass palette) mode fixes that. The first pass runs through your exact clip and generates a palette tuned to the colors that are actually in it; the second pass redraws every frame against that palette. A clip that is mostly one blue UI, or mostly one green field, gets 256 shades of what it needs instead of 256 shades of everything. It costs roughly double the processing time and it is worth it — that is why it is the default. Pick Fast (single pass) only when you want a rough preview quickly.

An honest note on limits

  • Nothing is uploaded. FFmpeg is compiled to WebAssembly and runs inside this page, so your footage never touches a server and there is no upload wait.
  • Very large files hit browser memory. We impose no cap, but the whole file is held in your browser's memory — roughly 1–2 GB on a desktop, considerably less on a phone. Beyond that the conversion fails rather than silently producing junk.
  • Converting is CPU-bound. Every frame is decoded and re-encoded, single-threaded here, so a few minutes for a long or wide clip is normal, not a bug.
  • First use downloads the ~31 MB engine once, then the browser caches it.
  • GIF has no audio. If sound matters, keep the clip as a video.

Great for

  • Reaction clips and short loops for chat apps like Slack, Discord and KakaoTalk
  • Showing a UI interaction inside a README, a pull request or a bug report, where video will not play inline
  • Documentation and help articles that need a silent, auto-playing demo
  • Forum and blog posts on platforms that accept images but not video

Where GIF is the wrong answer: anything long, anything with sound, anything full-screen, and anything where quality matters. For those, an MP4 is smaller and better looking — trim it to the part you need, or compress it to fit a size limit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my video really never uploaded?

Correct. The tool downloads FFmpeg compiled to WebAssembly (about 31 MB, once, then cached) and builds the GIF inside the browser. Nothing is transmitted, so there is no upload wait either — a 500 MB source file starts processing immediately.

Why is my GIF so huge, and how do I make it smaller?

GIF stores every frame as a separate image with none of the compression tricks modern video uses, so the size grows with width, frame rate and length all at once. A 640 px, 15 fps, 20-second clip can easily pass 30 MB. Cut the length first — staying under 10 seconds is the single biggest win — then drop the width to 480 or 320 px, then the frame rate to 12 or 8 fps.

What does the two-pass palette actually do?

A GIF can only hold 256 colors. Left alone, the encoder falls back to a generic palette and gradients turn into blotchy banding. The first pass scans your exact clip and builds a palette optimized for its colors, and the second pass redraws the frames using that palette. It takes roughly twice as long and looks dramatically better, which is why it is the default.

Why does my GIF have no sound?

The GIF format has no audio track at all — that is the file format, not a limitation of this tool. If you need sound, keep the clip as a video instead: trim it with our video trimmer, or shrink it with the video compressor.

How long does conversion take, and is there a size limit?

Converting re-encodes every frame, and the engine runs single-threaded here, so a long or wide clip can take a few minutes — the progress bar and the button show the real percentage as it works. We impose no size limit, but everything happens in your browser's memory, so the practical ceiling is roughly 1–2 GB on a desktop and less on a phone.
